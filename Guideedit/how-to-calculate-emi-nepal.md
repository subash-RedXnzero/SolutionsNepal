<h2 id="quick-answer">Quick Answer</h2>

EMI stands for <strong>Equated Monthly Instalment</strong> — the fixed amount you pay a bank every month on a reducing-balance loan. It is built from just three inputs: the loan amount, the annual interest rate, and the tenure in months.

Banks use this formula:

```text
EMI = P × r × (1 + r)ⁿ ÷ ((1 + r)ⁿ − 1)
```

Worked example — Rs 50,00,000 at 11% a year for 20 years:

- <strong>Monthly EMI</strong> — about Rs 51,609
- <strong>Total paid over 240 months</strong> — about Rs 1,23,86,261
- <strong>Interest in that total</strong> — about Rs 73,86,261
- <strong>Rule of thumb</strong> — about Rs 1,032 per lakh per month

To see how much loan your income supports, use the <a href="/nepal/finance/loan-eligibility-calculator-nepal">loan eligibility calculator</a>; for the upfront cash on a home purchase, the <a href="/nepal/finance/home-loan-down-payment-calculator-nepal">down payment calculator</a>.

The single most useful thing to understand about EMI is what it actually contains. On this loan, the first instalment of Rs 51,609 includes roughly <strong>Rs 45,833 of interest</strong> and only about <strong>Rs 5,776 of principal</strong> — nearly 89% of your first payment is the cost of the money, not the money itself. It gets better slowly: after five years you have repaid about Rs 4,59,303 of principal (and still owe about Rs 45,40,697), and after ten years about Rs 12,53,400 (still owing about Rs 37,46,600).

<h2 id="what-is-emi">What is EMI?</h2>

An EMI is the fixed monthly payment that clears both the money you borrowed and the interest on it, so that the loan reaches exactly zero at the end of the agreed tenure. Every instalment has two parts:

- <strong>Principal</strong> — the part that reduces what you owe
- <strong>Interest</strong> — the bank's charge on the balance still outstanding

The instalment stays the same every month, but the split inside it does not: early payments are mostly interest, later payments are mostly principal. That is why paying a little extra early in the loan is worth far more than the same extra amount paid near the end.

> **Key takeaway:** EMI is not a fee and not a separate charge — it is your principal plus interest, spread evenly across the tenure. A bank's "EMI" figure and its "monthly instalment" figure are the same thing.

<h2 id="emi-formula">The EMI Formula</h2>

The standard reducing-balance formula used by banks and finance companies in Nepal:

```text
EMI = P × r × (1 + r)ⁿ ÷ ((1 + r)ⁿ − 1)
```

What each symbol means:

- <strong>P</strong> — principal: the amount actually borrowed, after any down payment
- <strong>r</strong> — the monthly interest rate: annual rate ÷ 12 ÷ 100
- <strong>n</strong> — the number of monthly instalments: years × 12

The version most people meet in a bank is the "per lakh" table — how much EMI one lakh of loan costs per month at a given rate and tenure. Multiply that figure by the number of lakhs you are borrowing and you have your EMI. At 11% for 20 years it is about Rs 1,032 per lakh, which is how the Rs 51,609 figure above arises.

<h2 id="factors">What Changes Your EMI</h2>

Four things move the number, and three of them are negotiable before you sign:

- <strong>Loan amount</strong> — the larger the principal, the larger the EMI. This is the only input that changes your EMI in exact proportion.
- <strong>Interest rate</strong> — the annual rate, divided by 12 for the monthly calculation. Small rate differences are expensive over long tenures.
- <strong>Tenure</strong> — spread the same loan over more months and the EMI falls, but total interest rises. Tenure is the biggest lever most borrowers have.
- <strong>Down payment</strong> — money paid upfront is money you never borrow, so it cuts both the EMI and the total interest. It also affects the bank's loan-to-value check.

Two other things affect your monthly outflow but not the EMI itself: the bank's processing fee (charged once) and any insurance or product fee bundled with the loan.

<h2 id="how-to-calculate">Worked Example: Rs 50 Lakh at 11% for 20 Years</h2>

<h3 id="example">Step by Step</h3>

Assume a home loan of Rs 50,00,000 at 11% a year for 20 years. (11% is used here as a worked example only — read your bank's quoted rate, which is its own base rate plus a premium, before comparing.)

<strong>Step 1 — convert the annual rate to a monthly rate.</strong>

```text
r = 11 ÷ 12 ÷ 100 = 0.009167
```

<strong>Step 2 — count the instalments.</strong>

```text
n = 20 × 12 = 240 months
```

<strong>Step 3 — apply the formula.</strong>

```text
EMI = P × r × (1 + r)²⁴⁰ ÷ ((1 + r)²⁴⁰ − 1)
```

<strong>Step 4 — read the result.</strong>

```text
EMI ≈ Rs 51,609 per month
```

Cross-check it two ways. First, per lakh: Rs 51,609 ÷ 50 ≈ Rs 1,032, which matches standard EMI tables for 11% over 20 years. Second, totals: 240 instalments at about Rs 51,609 come to roughly Rs 1,23,86,261, of which about Rs 73,86,261 is interest and Rs 50,00,000 is the principal.

> **A note on rounding:** the total is calculated from the unrounded EMI, so total ÷ 240 can be a rupee or two away from the EMI the bank quotes you. Banks also round the EMI to the nearest rupee, which shifts the closing balance by a small amount. Both figures come from the same formula — neither is wrong.

<h2 id="tenure-cost">What a Longer Tenure Really Costs</h2>

Tenure is where most of the money is decided. Here is the same Rs 50,00,000 at the same 11%, stretched over five different tenures:

| Tenure | Monthly EMI | Total interest |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 5 years | Rs 1,08,712 | Rs 15,22,727 |
| 10 years | Rs 68,875 | Rs 32,65,001 |
| 15 years | Rs 56,830 | Rs 52,29,372 |
| 20 years | Rs 51,609 | Rs 73,86,261 |
| 25 years | Rs 49,006 | Rs 97,01,696 |

Read the bottom row against the middle. Going from 10 years to 20 years pulls the EMI down by about 25%, but adds about Rs 41 lakh of interest. Going from 20 to 25 years cuts the EMI by only about Rs 2,600 a month while adding about Rs 23 lakh of interest — at that point you are mostly buying time, not affordability.

The practical rule: choose the <strong>shortest tenure whose EMI you can genuinely pay every month, including in a bad month</strong>. If the EMI at a shorter tenure feels impossible on paper but you expect your income to rise, a slightly longer tenure with a habit of prepaying is a reasonable compromise — see the prepayment figures in the tips section.

<h2 id="rate-sensitivity">What One Percentage Point Does</h2>

On the same Rs 50,00,000 over 20 years:

| Annual rate | Monthly EMI | Total interest |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 9% | Rs 44,986 | Rs 57,96,711 |
| 10% | Rs 48,251 | Rs 65,80,260 |
| 11% | Rs 51,609 | Rs 73,86,261 |
| 12% | Rs 55,054 | Rs 82,13,034 |
| 13% | Rs 58,579 | Rs 90,58,909 |

One step of one percentage point — 11% to 12% — costs about Rs 3,445 more per month and about <strong>Rs 8,26,773 more interest</strong> across the loan. The whole 9% to 13% range is a difference of about Rs 32,62,198 in interest on the same house.

This is why rate shopping matters more than almost anything else on a long loan, and why a floating rate (which resets with your bank's base rate) is a real risk, not a footnote. Ask what the rate is today, what it is tied to, and how often it can move.

<h2 id="flat-vs-reducing">Flat Rate vs Reducing Balance</h2>

The formula above is the <strong>reducing-balance</strong> method, and it is the standard for home loans in Nepal. Some car, personal and hire-purchase quotes instead use a <strong>flat rate</strong>, where interest is charged on the full original amount for the entire tenure, no matter how much you have already repaid.

The two look similar on paper and are nothing alike in cost. A Rs 5,00,000 loan at a flat 12% over 36 months gives an EMI of about Rs 18,889 and total interest of Rs 1,80,000. A reducing-balance loan charging the same Rs 18,889 a month would carry an annual rate of roughly <strong>21.2%</strong> — more than nine points higher than the headline figure.

- <strong>Flat 12% headline</strong> — total interest Rs 1,80,000 on Rs 5,00,000 over 3 years
- <strong>Equivalent reducing balance</strong> — about 21.2% a year for the same EMI
- <strong>The question to ask</strong> — "Is this rate flat or reducing balance?" and "What is the annualised reducing rate?"

If a seller or dealer quotes you a monthly "instalment plan" price, ask for the flat-versus-reducing answer in writing, then compare it with a bank loan using the <a href="/nepal/finance/emi-calculator">EMI calculator</a>. The cheaper-looking option is often the more expensive one.

<h2 id="nepal-checks">What to Check With a Nepali Bank</h2>

An EMI quote is the start of the conversation, not the end. Before signing:

- <strong>Base rate plus premium</strong> — most Nepali loan rates are floating and priced as the bank's base rate plus a premium. Ask for both numbers, in writing, so you know what moves and by how much.
- <strong>Reset frequency</strong> — ask whether the EMI or the tenure changes when the rate resets, and how often resets happen.
- <strong>Processing and documentation fees</strong> — one-off charges, usually a percentage of the loan amount. They do not change the EMI, but they change the cost of borrowing.
- <strong>Insurance and bundled products</strong> — some loans require property or credit insurance. Check whether it is required and what it costs per year.
- <strong>Prepayment rules</strong> — whether part-payment is allowed, how often, the minimum amount, and any charge. Ask how prepayment is applied: to reduce the EMI or to shorten the tenure.
- <strong>Amortisation schedule</strong> — ask for the full month-by-month table showing interest, principal and closing balance. Any bank can produce it, and it settles most arguments later.
- <strong>Rate type</strong> — fixed for how long, then what? A "fixed for the first year" loan is a floating loan with a delayed start.
- <strong>Loan-to-value limit</strong> — how much of the property value the bank will fund. This decides your down payment, and therefore your EMI.

Rates, fees and product rules change with every monetary-policy cycle and with each bank's own policy. Confirm the current figures with your bank and with <a href="https://www.nrb.org.np" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Nepal Rastra Bank</a> publications rather than relying on any table, including this one.

<h2 id="tips">How to Reduce Your EMI</h2>

- <strong>Increase the down payment</strong> — every rupee you pay upfront is a rupee you never pay interest on. It lowers the EMI and the total interest at once.
- <strong>Negotiate the rate before the tenure</strong> — one percentage point off a 20-year loan is worth more than several years of tenure juggling. Compare at least three banks.
- <strong>Prepay whenever you have surplus cash</strong> — on the Rs 50 lakh, 20-year example above, adding Rs 10,000 a month to the EMI clears the loan in about 150 months (12.5 years) and cuts total interest from about Rs 73,86,261 to about Rs 41,98,148 — a saving of roughly Rs 31.9 lakh. Ask the bank to apply prepayments to the principal and to shorten the tenure.
- <strong>Keep your credit profile clean</strong> — repay credit-card balances and other loans before applying, because your income-to-EMI ratio drives the rate and the approval.
- <strong>Consider a balance transfer</strong> — if another bank offers a materially lower rate and the transfer cost is small, moving the loan can save lakhs. Do the arithmetic including the transfer fee before deciding.
- <strong>Choose the shorter tenure you can actually sustain</strong> — an EMI you can pay comfortably beats a stretched one that turns into a penalty.
- <strong>Read the reset clause</strong> — on a floating loan, budgeting for a higher EMI later is safer than assuming today's rate lasts 20 years.

<h2 id="mistakes">Common Mistakes</h2>

- <strong>Comparing EMIs without comparing total interest.</strong> A lower EMI usually means more interest, not a better deal.
- <strong>Assuming a flat-rate quote is a reducing-balance rate.</strong> A flat 12% can be equivalent to a reducing rate above 20%.
- <strong>Forgetting the fees.</strong> Processing, insurance and documentation charges add real cost even though they never appear in the EMI.
- <strong>Ignoring the reset clause on a floating loan.</strong>
- <strong>Treating the EMI as the maximum you can afford.</strong> Budget with a margin; income and expenses both move.
- <strong>Skipping the amortisation schedule.</strong> It is the only document that shows exactly where your money goes each month.
- <strong>Repaying a long loan and a short loan at random.</strong> Extra payments save the most on the highest-rate loan.

<h2 id="conclusion">The Bottom Line</h2>

EMI is simple arithmetic wrapped in a long commitment: a fixed monthly payment that covers principal plus interest on the outstanding balance, calculated from the amount, the rate and the tenure. Understand which part of each payment is interest, compare total interest rather than the monthly figure, ask whether the quote is flat or reducing balance, and check the reset clause before you sign. A few minutes with the <a href="/nepal/finance/emi-calculator">EMI calculator</a> before visiting the bank is usually worth more than an hour of negotiating after.
