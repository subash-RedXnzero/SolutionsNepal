<h2 id="quick-answer">Quick Answer</h2>

Almost every percentage problem in daily life is one of three questions:

1. <strong>What is X% of a number?</strong> — a discount, a tax, a commission, a raise.
2. <strong>What percentage is one number of another?</strong> — exam marks, a budget line, a tax rate.
3. <strong>By what percentage did something change?</strong> — price rise, salary increase, population growth.

Underneath all three is one idea: <strong>a percentage is a fraction with 100 as the denominator</strong>. "15%" simply means 15 out of every 100, or 0.15.

- 15% of Rs 4,000 = <strong>Rs 600</strong>
- 45 marks out of 60 = <strong>75%</strong>
- Salary from Rs 30,000 to Rs 34,500 = <strong>+15%</strong>
- Rs 800 after a 20% discount means the original price was <strong>Rs 1,000</strong>

<h2 id="three-questions">The Three Percentage Questions</h2>

Practising the three shapes separately is what makes percentages finally click, because the words in the question tell you which formula to use:

- "of" usually means multiply.
- "out of" or "per" usually means divide.
- "increase", "decrease", "change" or "growth" means you need an original value to compare against.

Once you can name which of the three you are facing, you no longer need to remember separate tricks for marks, discounts and tax.

<h2 id="formulas">The Formulas</h2>

Finding a percentage of a number:

```text
Result = number × percent ÷ 100
```

Finding what percentage one number is of another:

```text
Percent = (part ÷ total) × 100
```

Finding percentage change:

```text
Change % = (new − old) ÷ old × 100
```

Finding the original value after a percentage change:

```text
Original = final ÷ (1 ± rate)
```

The last one is the one people forget. If a price rose 25% to Rs 1,250, the original was 1,250 ÷ 1.25 = Rs 1,000 — <em>not</em> 1,250 − 25%.

<h2 id="examples">Worked Examples</h2>

| Situation | Working | Answer |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 45 marks out of 60 | (45 ÷ 60) × 100 | 75% |
| Rs 5,000 tax on Rs 50,000 income | (5,000 ÷ 50,000) × 100 | 10% |
| Rs 800 after 20% discount — original? | 800 ÷ 0.80 | Rs 1,000 |
| Rs 2,700 after 10% discount — original? | 2,700 ÷ 0.90 | Rs 3,000 |
| 8 kg of a 50 kg sack | (8 ÷ 50) × 100 | 16% |
| Rs 4,000 rent rising to Rs 4,500 | (4,500 − 4,000) ÷ 4,000 × 100 | +12.5% |
| Rs 1,200 marked down to Rs 900 | (900 − 1,200) ÷ 1,200 × 100 | −25% |
| 12% service charge on Rs 2,500 | 2,500 × 0.12 | Rs 300 |

<h2 id="mental-math">Mental Maths Shortcuts</h2>

Most everyday percentages can be done in your head once you can build them from 10% and 1%:

- **10%** — move the decimal point one place left. 10% of Rs 1,850 is Rs 185.
- **5%** — half of the 10% value. Rs 92.50.
- **1%** — move the decimal two places left. Rs 18.50.
- **15%** — 10% + 5% = Rs 185 + Rs 92.50 = Rs 277.50.
- **20%** — divide by 5, or double the 10% value. Rs 370.
- **25%** — divide by 4. Rs 462.50.
- **50%** — half the number. Rs 925.
- **75%** — half plus a quarter. Rs 1,387.50.

To find the price after a discount, you can also work with what remains: 20% off means you pay 80%, so multiply by 0.8. This is faster than finding the discount and subtracting it, and it avoids one subtraction error.

<h2 id="marks">Percentages for Marks and Grades in Nepal</h2>

For a single subject or a whole exam on a marks system, the calculation is direct:

- <strong>Marks percentage</strong> = marks obtained ÷ full marks × 100.
- <strong>Aggregate percentage</strong> = total marks obtained ÷ total full marks × 100. Do not average the subject percentages — that only works when every subject has the same full marks.

Nepal's school system, however, reports <strong>letter grades and GPA</strong> for SEE and for +2 (NEB), not raw percentages. That creates a genuine trap: there is no single official formula that converts a GPA back into a percentage, because the grade bands are published by the board and can change between years.

What to do instead:

- Read your <strong>grade sheet</strong> — it already shows the grade and grade point per subject.
- If an institution asks for a percentage equivalent, ask it to state the conversion it accepts, rather than assuming one.
- Check the current <strong>NEB</strong> notices at <a href="https://neb.gov.np" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">neb.gov.np</a> for the grade bands for your year.
- For our own worked explainers on the grading tables, see the <a href="/blog/see-gpa-calculator-nepal-grading-percentage-guide">SEE GPA guide</a> and the <a href="/blog/neb-gpa-calculator-nepal-class-11-12-percentage-guide">NEB class 11–12 GPA guide</a>, or use the <a href="/nepal/education/gpa-percentage">GPA to percentage tool</a>.

<h2 id="discounts-vat">Discounts and 13% VAT</h2>

Discounts and VAT are both percentage calculations, and Nepali shoppers run into both in the same transaction — especially during Dashain and Tihar sales.

<strong>Discounts.</strong> "25% off" means you pay 75% of the ticket price. A Rs 2,400 jacket at 25% off costs 2,400 × 0.75 = Rs 1,800. Two consecutive discounts do not simply add: 20% off then an extra 10% off is 0.8 × 0.9 = 0.72, which is a 28% total discount, not 30%.

<strong>VAT.</strong> Nepal's standard VAT rate is 13%, administered by the Inland Revenue Department (verify the current rate and any changes at <a href="https://ird.gov.np" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">ird.gov.np</a>):

- **Adding VAT** — multiply by 1.13. Rs 5,000 + 13% VAT = Rs 5,650.
- **Extracting VAT** — from a tax-inclusive price, divide by 1.13. Rs 5,650 inclusive means a base of Rs 5,000 and VAT of Rs 650.
- **The VAT component alone** — inclusive price × 13 ÷ 113. For Rs 5,650 that is Rs 650.

A common error is applying 13% to an already VAT-inclusive amount to "find the VAT", which overstates it. Always check whether the quoted price already includes VAT.

<h2 id="increase-decrease">Increases and Decreases — Salary, Rent, Prices</h2>

Percentage change always needs two values and a direction:

- **Salary** — from Rs 30,000 to Rs 34,500 is (34,500 − 30,000) ÷ 30,000 × 100 = <strong>+15%</strong>.
- **Rent** — from Rs 18,000 to Rs 20,000 is (2,000 ÷ 18,000) × 100 = <strong>+11.1%</strong>.
- **Price** — from Rs 1,200 to Rs 900 is (−300 ÷ 1,200) × 100 = <strong>−25%</strong>.
- **Reversing an increase** — a figure that grew 25% to Rs 1,250 came from 1,250 ÷ 1.25 = <strong>Rs 1,000</strong>.
- **Reversing a decrease** — a figure that fell 15% to Rs 1,700 came from 1,700 ÷ 0.85 = <strong>Rs 2,000</strong>.

A useful warning: a 50% fall followed by a 50% rise does not return you to the start. Rs 1,000 → Rs 500 → Rs 750. Losses and gains are calculated on different bases, which is why recovering from a loss needs a bigger percentage than the loss itself.

<h2 id="percentage-points">Percent vs Percentage Points — The Costliest Mistake</h2>

Two numbers that look identical in a headline can mean very different things:

- Interest rising from <strong>10% to 12%</strong> is a change of <strong>2 percentage points</strong> — but a <strong>20% increase</strong> in the rate itself, because 2 ÷ 10 = 0.20.
- A tax rate moving from <strong>5% to 6%</strong> is <strong>1 percentage point</strong>, or a <strong>20% increase</strong>.

This distinction matters most when reading news about interest rates, tax rates, exam pass rates and election results. If a headline says "rates up 2%", check whether it means 2 percentage points or a 2% relative increase — the difference can be large.

<h2 id="mistakes">Common Mistakes</h2>

- <strong>Adding percentages that apply in sequence.</strong> 20% then 10% is 28%, not 30%.
- <strong>Reversing an increase by subtracting the same percentage.</strong> Rs 1,250 minus 25% is Rs 937.50, not Rs 1,000.
- <strong>Averaging percentages of different totals.</strong> Weigh them by their totals, or you will get a figure that matches nothing.
- <strong>Confusing percent and percentage points.</strong>
- <strong>Using the wrong base.</strong> "50% of the increase" and "a 50% increase" are different things.
- <strong>Forgetting that the discount base may exclude VAT</strong>, or vice versa — always confirm what the quoted price includes.

<h2 id="sources">Sources and Disclaimer</h2>

This is general arithmetic guidance. Rates, thresholds and grade bands change, so confirm anything that affects money or admission decisions with the authority that publishes it.

- **Inland Revenue Department** — current VAT rate and rules: <a href="https://ird.gov.np" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">ird.gov.np</a>
- **National Examination Board** — SEE and +2 grading: <a href="https://neb.gov.np" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">neb.gov.np</a>
- **Conversion factors** — percentage × total ÷ 100 for "percent of", and the formulas above for change and reversal.

For anything with a legal or financial consequence — a tax return, a contract price, an admission application — use the official rate and keep the working in writing.
